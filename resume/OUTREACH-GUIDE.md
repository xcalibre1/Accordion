# Outreach guide

Each version directory has an `outreach-email.md` with a ready-to-send draft. This file covers the parts that are the same regardless of company.

## Sequence matters: apply first, then email

Apply through the official careers portal **before** emailing anyone. Then the email references an existing application instead of asking the manager to do a recruiter's job. This matters most at Doctolib, which publishes a structured process (recruiter screen → live coding → system design → behavioural → reference check) and asks applicants to exclude photos and age; going around that looks like you did not read it. Going *alongside* it is fine and often what gets an application pulled out of the pile.

## Finding the right person

In order of preference:

1. **The hiring manager or engineering lead for that specific team.** Search LinkedIn for the company plus "Engineering Manager", "Engineering Lead" or "Staff Engineer", and filter by the office location. Then read their profile or recent posts for the team name from the posting — DIAL or Phone Assistant at Doctolib, Bank Web or Banking at SumUp. A generic manager at a 7,000-person company is not much better than the portal; the right squad's manager is worth a lot.
2. **The recruiter named on the posting**, if there is one. Lower ceiling but much higher response rate, and they can route you.
3. **An engineer on the team.** Ask a genuine question about the product rather than asking for a referral in the first message. Engineers answer product questions from people who clearly read their job posting.

## Getting the address

Most European tech companies use `firstname.lastname@company.com`, but **do not guess blindly** — a bounce is a wasted shot, and a wrong-person hit is worse. Verify with a tool like Hunter.io before sending.

Honestly, **LinkedIn usually beats email** for cold outreach to a manager: your message arrives in the same place they already handle recruiting, and it carries your profile with it. Use email when you have a verified address, LinkedIn otherwise. Each `outreach-email.md` includes a shortened LinkedIn variant, because connection-request notes cap at 300 characters and InMail gets skimmed harder than email.

## What makes these drafts work

- **Short.** Under 200 words. A manager triages this on a phone between meetings.
- **One proof point, not a biography.** The resume is attached for the rest. Each draft leads with the single most relevant thing and a number attached to it.
- **Specific to the team.** Naming the squad and what it owns proves you read past the salary section. This is most of what separates a cold email that gets read from one that gets deleted.
- **The obvious objection is answered, not avoided.** Relocation for Berlin and Sofia; the missing LLM experience for Doctolib. A manager who spots a gap you did not mention assumes you are hiding it. A manager who sees you name it and act on it reads the rest of your application differently.
- **A low-cost ask.** "Worth a conversation?" is easier to say yes to than "please interview me."
- **No adjectives about yourself.** Not passionate, not detail-oriented, not a fast learner. Claims about character are free to make and therefore worthless; the numbers do that work instead.

## Timing and follow-up

Send Tuesday to Thursday, morning in the recipient's time zone — so before roughly 11:00 CET for Berlin and Sofia, which is early afternoon IST.

Follow up **once**, five to seven working days later, with two or three lines that add something rather than repeating yourself: a relevant thing you shipped that week, or one specific question about the team. If that gets no reply, stop. Silence is usually workload, not judgment, and a third message converts indifference into a decision.

## Before you send any of these

- Replace every `[Name]` and `[Team]` placeholder. An unedited placeholder is an instant delete.
- Attach the right PDF from the matching version directory.
- Re-read any sentence describing something you are "currently building" and delete it if you have not started. These drafts are only useful because they are checkable, and a manager who asks one follow-up question about work that does not exist has learned something much worse than a skills gap.
